Part one · Technology

Filters guard the door. Habits shape the heart.

Safeguards like filters, screen-time limits and accountability software matter, so keep them. But a filter only answers what our teens see. It can't answer who they are becoming.

Protection

  • Blocks harmful content
  • Limits time and access
  • Works while we're watching
  • Ends when they leave home

Formation

  • Shapes desires and habits
  • Builds attention, patience, self-control
  • Works when no one is watching
  • Goes with them for life

A teen can have a perfectly filtered phone and still be trained by it: to reach for it every few minutes, to need constant novelty, to measure worth in likes, and to escape every moment of boredom or discomfort. None of that is "bad content." All of it is formation.

The attention economy

If the app is free, your teen is the product.

Social apps make money by selling advertisers access to our attention and data. Advertisers are the customer. The more time your teen spends in the app, the more it earns, so every design choice pushes toward more.

"My experience is what I agree to attend to."William James, 1890, a line Catherine Price builds on in How to Break Up with Your Phone

Spot the hook — tap a card to see how it works

Try this at home: ask your teen to find three of these tricks in their favorite app. Teens push back on being controlled, but they hate being manipulated. This turns "parents vs. me" into "us vs. the people trying to use us."

The new frontier

What is generative AI, and where do chatbots fit?

Generative AI is technology that creates new content: writing, images, voices, even video. It learns patterns from enormous amounts of text and media, then predicts what should come next. It doesn't "know" things the way a person does, and it can sound confident while being completely wrong.

Chatbots are the conversational kind

ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, Snapchat's My AI and others let you type or talk to the AI as if it were a person. Teens mostly use them in two ways:

  • As a tool: help with homework, explaining a concept, brainstorming. Think of it like a very fast, sometimes-wrong calculator for words.
  • As a companion: an AI designed to be a friend or confidant. This is where parents should pay the most attention.

It's already in their apps. AI chat is built into Snapchat, Instagram, WhatsApp and many games, so your teen may be using it without downloading anything new.

The discernment connection: a chatbot is an "author" too. The same questions apply. Can it be wrong? Does it line up with Scripture? Who made it, and what do they want?

Conversation starters (curious, not accusing)

  • "Which AI tools do your friends use? What do they use them for?"
  • "Have you ever talked to an AI about something personal? How did it respond?"
  • "What's the difference between a friend who always agrees with you and one who tells you the truth?" (Proverbs 27:6)
  • "If an app is free and always available to listen, what do you think the company gets out of it?"

Teach the why, not just the rule

Before you pick up the phone…

Catherine Price suggests three questions to ask before unlocking a phone. Simple enough for a whole family to memorize.

What for?

What am I picking this up to do? A purpose, or just a reflex?

Why now?

Am I bored, anxious, avoiding something? What am I really feeling?

What else?

What could I do instead: read, call a friend, go outside, pray, rest?

Part two · Books

This session is about books and technology. At a deeper level, it's about discernment.

"Study to show thyself approved" (2 Timothy 2:15). The word means be diligent. Helping our kids read well is part of that diligence.

Why stories matter, even for older kids

Brain scans showed that children who were read to more at home had stronger activity in areas for mental imagery and for understanding words and stories.

Cincinnati Children's study, 2015 (preschoolers ages 3–5)

"The single most important activity for building the knowledge required for eventual success in reading is reading aloud to children."

Becoming a Nation of Readers, 1985

"Across 70 experiments, novels did more to boost concern for and understanding of others."

Adam Grant, organizational psychologist, "The Case for Reading Fiction," 2025

"When God contemplated all the possible ways… to transmit and preserve his revelation to the world, he chose a book."

John Piper

Not just reading — interacting. There's a difference between reading the words on a page and engaging with your child about the words on the page.

Why read fiction?

It shares timeless truth

Great books show us human nature and how good triumphs over evil. We cheer for Quasimodo because we know what it's like to feel like an outcast. We love Frodo because he perseveres against great evil.

It trains us to see through people

A villain like Count Fosco in The Woman in White sticks with us because we've all met someone with a polished veneer who is deceitful underneath.

It builds compassion

On a screen, you observe Katniss or the March sisters. In a book, you get inside their heads. Slowly, that builds the capacity to see perspectives that aren't yours.

The number one thing

Discernment, discernment, discernment.

This is what we most want to pass on to our kids as they read.

Authors can be wrong.

Every book outside the Bible is written by a fallible person, even favorites like C.S. Lewis, Paul Tripp and A.W. Tozer. We always come to a book asking, "What does Scripture say?"

Viewpoints should align with Scripture.

Look at morally gray characters, flaws, and good versus evil through the lens of Scripture. Does good triumph? Is the author clear about good and evil? Is a viewpoint that contradicts Scripture presented positively?

Good and evil should be clear.

In The Lord of the Rings, it's clear who the good guy is and who the bad guy is. And it's clear why.

The same questions work for tech. YouTubers, influencers, podcasters and AI chatbots are "authors" too. Teach your teen to ask of every creator: Can they be wrong? Does this line up with Scripture? Is it honest about good and evil?
So what does this mean for your family?

Six habits that build readers

Habit 1

Read in front of your teen

If we don't value reading, our kids won't either. Let them see you reading for pleasure: not for work or a Bible study, but because you chose it.

It doesn't matter much what you read. It matters that they see you do it, especially as phones take more of our downtime.

Habit 2

Read with your teen

Talk about books together. "Buy your child a book every month and take them on a date to discuss it. Talk about the characters like they're real people. Argue about their decisions." (shared on Instagram by @the_girl_zora_)

Not only Bible studies, but books about culture, classics and biographies.

Habit 3

Read to them, yes, even teens

Use the voices. Read some books a little above their level. Don't stop when they learn to read on their own.

Reading aloud lets you talk through harder books, like Pride and Prejudice or The Count of Monte Cristo, that teens often get bogged down in alone.

Habit 4

Let them choose

Encourage quality, but it's okay if your middle schooler loves graphic novels or your daughter loves fantasy. They don't have to like the same books you do.

Habit 5

Take them to the library

Weekly when they're small, a couple of times a month as teens. Investing in reading doesn't stop in elementary school.

Habit 6

Start a family reading time

Everyone sits together and reads their own book. Pair it with an evening screen cutoff: "We're all going to read now."

"My dad read to us all the way until we were teenagers. I still remember those evenings as some of the best times of my life. And I remember the books too."

How much can 15 minutes a day add up to? John Piper's math: 200 words a minute × 15 minutes × 365 days ≈ 1.1 million words, about 12 substantial books a year. Try the calculator.
